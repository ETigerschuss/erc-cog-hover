# Abstract (Part A, max 2000 characters incl. spaces)

<!-- ABSTRACT START -->
Animal visual systems are tuned to the tasks animals must solve, but which circuit features exist because of a task is hard to tell, since species differ in too much at once. Sexual dimorphism removes this confound. Male hoverflies (Eristalis tenax) pursue mates in fast aerial chases, and their visual system differs from the female one at every level: a high-acuity dorso-frontal zone, a sexually dimorphic motion-sensitive neuron (HSN), and a male-only third retinal muscle that moves the retina under the lens. HOVER asks which circuit motifs make a visual system task-specific, and whether those motifs carry over to engineered spiking vision.
We will (1) build connectome-constrained spiking neural network models of the fly motion-vision and gaze pathway, from photoreceptors to eye movement, that for the first time include electrical synapses, identified with our MESH gap-junction pipeline in the Drosophila connectome, at three levels: biophysical, mathematically reduced, and trainable; (2) map these models onto male and female hoverflies and test competing hypotheses with optimised controversial stimuli and retinal-movement recordings; (3) train the models on pursuit versus stabilisation tasks to test whether the male-specific features emerge as task-optimal solutions; (4) transfer the extracted motifs to event-camera spiking vision with active sensor micro-movements, benchmarked against deep networks for small-target detection, pursuit and ego-motion; and (5) run every model on low-cost spiking robots in computer-vision courses and in a distributed citizen-science experiment.
HOVER will deliver the first photon-to-eye-movement connectome model, a mechanistic and normative explanation of a visual sexual dimorphism, and open, tested design principles for efficient active machine vision.
